Learning policy-relative intents: what the experiments actually show

A guided account of the five-stage local campaign, with the actual boards, intent formats, model outputs and prompts needed to interpret its results.

Campaign: 15 November 2026 · Exploratory evaluation · Proposal source: 15 November pre-second-review ZIP · Original compact report

Reading guide

The main distinction

The proposal studies an intent generator that requests an intermediate outcome from a fixed controller. The interesting question is whether controller measurements help the generator select requests, and whether a relative temporal scale adds value beyond simpler alternatives.

The campaign contains useful pieces of that question, but it does not yet join them into the proposed system. Qwen generated structured intents and was checked with a local solver. Separately, older full-state controllers and planners were evaluated using complete target boards.

1 · An intent

“Put a box at row 2, column 4”, possibly together with other desired conditions and conditions to preserve.

2 · A controller

Acts for a request. The CLLP in these experiments receives an entire target board; it is not an intent-conditioned controller.

3 · A planner

Combines intermediate requests. The complete-task comparison uses older full-state AdaSubS, not the proposal’s learnt relative-scale planner.

What is still open: these results do not establish that controller-conditioned training, relative scale u, or predicate intents improve complete-task planning. The evaluation boards were previously inspected, and the earlier checkpoints’ training exposure is unknown.

The report follows the terminology in the latest proposal. Intent is the structured request; its desired effects and any preservation conditions are parts of that intent. A demonstration offset k, the controller’s measured duration d, and the relative scale u are three different quantities.

01 · Representation

What does an intent look like?

The Sokoban implementation represents one intent as a JSON object with an array of desired effects, an array of preservation conditions and an optional instruction. The controller is supposed to achieve the requested effects together, while preserving any conditions throughout the attempt. There is no persistent box identity: box_at means that any box occupies the tile.

Field or predicateMeaningExample
effectsNon-empty array of desired predicates. Every desired predicate must initially be false for this experiment’s non-triviality check.[{…}, {…}] requests both at once.
box_atAny box occupies the specified tile.{"predicate":"box_at","row":2,"col":4}
player_atThe player occupies the specified tile.{"predicate":"player_at","row":3,"col":5}
boxes_on_goals_at_leastAt least the specified number of boxes simultaneously occupy goal tiles.{"predicate":"boxes_on_goals_at_least","count":2}
preserveConditions that must remain true; an empty array is legitimate. The demonstration checker verifies them over the recorded fragment.[]
instructionOptional natural-language rendering. In many saved examples it is null.null

Coordinates are zero-based: row counts from the top, column from the left. A box on a goal still satisfies box_at. A valid JSON object may nevertheless be invalid as an intent: a coordinate can be a wall, a requested count can exceed the number of boxes, or an effect can already hold.

Three different outcomes. Valid means the syntax and initial predicate checks pass. Path found by bounded search means a separate solver found and replayed a sequence satisfying the intent within its limits. Controller achievement means the fixed controller itself achieved the intent. These are not interchangeable, and none alone means that the whole puzzle was solved.

02 · Data

Where the training intents came from

A recorded trajectory is a sequence of observations and primitive actions. For annotation, the pipeline compares an initial observation with a later one and constructs predicates that were false before and true afterwards. It checks the recorded fragment and retains provenance. The later observation and recorded actions are evidence used for annotation: they are not inputs to the generator at test time.

What is an independent board?

A layout describes the fixed walls and goal positions. A trajectory through one layout can supply many overlapping fragments. Rotating or reflecting it creates another view, not a new independent puzzle. The campaign groups layouts and their symmetries before assigning splits; uncertainty is calculated over original boards, not over fragments or transformed views.

For example, the initial 14 November generator study had 100 test contexts from 20 boards. That means five contexts per board on average, not 100 unrelated puzzles. In the newer LoRA study, NARROW used 256 training examples from 9 layouts; WIDE used 256 from 64 layouts. Its validation set had 100 examples from 10 separate layouts. The evaluation used 24 original boards per family, each also examined in two transformed orientations.

The 4-box evaluation boards come from RL validation data; the 10-box set mixes gamma2 and reverse-generated sources. These sources differ in more than box count. Their comparison is therefore not an isolated measurement of the effect of adding boxes. The reported validation/test roles describe the campaign’s split; old-model pretraining exposure cannot be verified.

05 · Stage 1

Can a frozen controller reach a demonstrated target board?

For each selected trajectory context, the experiment supplied CLLP with the current board and a complete board recorded k primitive actions later. It then allowed at most 32 controller actions and recorded whether the exact target board was reached. The target board came from the demonstration and was explicitly provided to CLLP. A push is one kind of primitive action; k is not a push count, a shortest-path distance or the proposal’s relative scale.

The campaign completed 11,760/11,760 planned trials across five CLLP checkpoints and the separate direct policy. The selected results below concern CLLP-8 and CLLP-32. A trajectory too short to provide a given offset is absent from that offset’s denominator, not counted as a failure.

06 · Stage 2

What is policy-relative temporal scale?

The proposal defines scale within a single context: start from the same restored state and controller memory, try several candidate intents, and describe their durations relative to the other successful candidates in that context. It is an empirical property of this controller, candidate pool and action limit, not an intrinsic label attached to a board or a synonym for demonstration offset.

Same context, candidate intents→Controller attempts→Success fractions p and durations d→Weighted relative scale u

For candidate j, let pj be its observed success fraction and dj the mean number of actions on successful attempts. For candidates with at least one success, weights are proportional to pj. Their weighted duration distribution gives the midpoint percentile, including ties:

wj = pj / Σk pk;  Fc(t) = Σj wj 1[dj ≤ t];  uj = [Fc(dj−) + Fc(dj)] / 2.

As a hypothetical illustration, three equally reliable candidates with successful durations 2, 8 and 12 actions receive scales 1/6, 1/2 and 5/6. If two durations tie, they receive the same midpoint. A candidate with no success gets no duration or scale label; its failed attempts still count in reliability and cost. One successful candidate alone gives u = 0.5 and cannot establish a short-versus-long distinction.

What was actually measured here: stage 2 used complete target boards with the frozen CLLP, not predicate intents with an intent-conditioned controller. It computed analogous profiles to investigate the measurement, not to train or evaluate the proposal’s scale-conditioned generator.

There were 8,576 evaluation attempts and 438 training-role attempts. Of the evaluation attempts, 950 did not reach their target within 32 actions. On the narrower subset of demonstration targets reached by both controllers, ranks based on measured duration and demonstration offset were very close: mean absolute differences were 0.003–0.005 across the four family/controller combinations. Strict inversions ranged from 2/894 to 10/2,035 comparable pairs.

That near-agreement is conditional on controller-selected successes. It does not say that failures carry no information, nor that the same agreement holds for arbitrary generated intents. Changing the candidate pool or action limit also changed the empirical scales. With deterministic CLLP and one attempt per candidate, observed success is a hit or miss, not a precise estimate of stochastic reliability.

07 · Stage 3

Does Qwen use the current board?

The concern was that a model might output a familiar coordinate regardless of the current state. Two checks probe that association. First, saved intents were reassigned to other boards within the same source family; this preserves the frequency of model outputs while changing which board they accompany. Second, the same layouts were rotated or reflected and the generated coordinates mapped back. Many different intents can be valid on one board, so disagreement after transformation is a diagnostic rather than proof that the model has no understanding of the state.

Fresh generation produced 432/432 planned responses across 48 original boards, three orientations and three methods. On the 24 original 4-box boards, the earlier LoRA produced 17 valid intents in the original orientation, 10 after rotation and 5 after reflection. These are paired views of the same layouts, not 72 independent boards.

On those same 4-box boards, the earlier LoRA’s original outputs were valid in 17/24 cases. After reassigning them to other boards in the same source, 24/72 checks were valid across three fixed permutations of those same 24 layouts. This decrease is consistent with some dependence on the paired board in this cohort. The 72 checks reuse the original responses and are not 72 new model calls or independent boards. Results differ by source: for example, on the 12 gamma2 10-box boards the corresponding counts were 9/12 original and 23/36 permuted. These observations do not by themselves establish useful planning.

The two-example ICL run on the 24 mixed-source 10-box boards produced 24/24 valid non-trivial intents, but all 24 requested the entire puzzle goal. Bounded BFS found a path for 0/24 under its fixed limit. A syntactically valid request can thus be too broad for the intended intermediate role; a cutoff remains unresolved.

08 · Stage 4

More layouts and coordinate transformations

All new adapters start from the same Qwen3-0.6B and use the same training seed, LoRA setup, update budget and validation procedure. They were trained on checked 4-box demonstration annotations, not controller outcomes. NARROW uses 256 examples from 9 layouts. WIDE uses 256 examples from 64 layouts. WIDE + D4 uses the same original examples as WIDE, with board and intent rotated or reflected consistently during training.

The models completed 150 updates; validation selected step 100 for NARROW and step 150 for WIDE and WIDE + D4. These are one-seed pilots. NARROW and WIDE share only 19 record IDs, so their comparison changes examples as well as layout count. The historical LoRA is useful context but was not trained under the matched new protocol.

The new adapters used rank-4 LoRA on the attention q_proj and v_proj modules, learning rate 0.0002, microbatch 1 and gradient accumulation 4. Fitting used the local RTX 3050 Ti laptop GPU in BF16; evaluation used the CPU. No low-level controller was retrained.

Results on original orientations

Each method produced one output per board. “Valid” requires a parseable, non-trivial intent. “Path found” means the separate bounded solver found a sequence, with a limit of 16 primitive actions, 2,500 expanded nodes and a five-second safety timeout. Invalid outputs stay in the denominator.

For example, 14/24 valid for WIDE means 14 of its 24 proposed intents passed the initial checks; it does not mean that 14 puzzles were solved. Of its 24 4-box requests, bounded search found paths for 13. A generator can improve this number by proposing easier intents, so the result does not by itself measure task progress.

Adding D4 transformations produced more conjunctions, but every parseable WIDE + D4 output still contained a box_at predicate. On 4-box boards, WIDE versus WIDE + D4 produced a single box_at in 22/24 versus 15/24 outputs; on 10-box boards the counts were 24/24 versus 11/24. Some added predicates were already true initially, making the conjunction invalid under the experiment’s rule: 5/9 augmented conjunctions on 4-box and 8/13 on 10-box boards. Validity intervals for WIDE + D4 minus WIDE included zero in both families.

09 · Stage 5

Can existing components solve complete puzzles?

This experiment begins from full puzzle starts, rather than intermediate demonstration fragments. It compares existing full-state components under the same per-board cap of 512 simulator transitions and 180 seconds. Each of the 20 selected boards per family was paired across all four methods. The direct policy receives the current board only. AdaSubS and fixed-k kSubS propose complete target boards and use the same frozen CLLP and value model; primitive A* is a secondary baseline.

@@PLANNER_ROWS@@
FamilyMethodFull puzzles solved / 20TimeoutsTotal transitionsGenerator calls

On the 20 ten-box boards, AdaSubS solved 18 and fixed-k kSubS solved 7. The paired difference is +55 percentage points, with a 95% board-bootstrap interval of +35 to +75 points for this exploratory cohort. AdaSubS used 5,456 transitions and 369 generator calls across the 20 boards; kSubS used 2,977 transitions and 179 calls. Equal transition caps do not equalise neural computation. The saved comparison includes two kSubS timeouts.

To show where work went, AdaSubS proposed 1,476 target boards on the ten-box set, of which 936 passed target validation. Its CLLP reached 613 targets in 753 completed controller attempts; 84 no-ops were recorded. Fixed-k kSubS had 505 valid targets among 716 proposals, 326 hits among 400 completed attempts and 46 no-ops. These denominators differ because invalid targets are not controller attempts, and truncated attempts are not completed attempts.

The ten-box source breakdown also matters: AdaSubS solved 8/10 gamma2 and 10/10 reverse-generated boards; kSubS solved 5/10 and 2/10. Reverse-generated starts had differing numbers of boxes already on goals, whereas these gamma2 starts had none. All selected starting puzzles were unsolved. This is a descriptive baseline on different source distributions, not a clean causal test of box count.

The proposal’s planner instead orders entries by the requested next expansion scale and value, key(n,u) = (u,Vψ(xn,g)), then tries intents from restored states and returns a full plan on task success. Stage 5 does not implement that learnt-scale mechanism, and its fixed demonstration horizons must not be renamed u.

10 · Earlier work

How the previous runs fit in

This HTML explains the five-stage local campaign and links to selected earlier work. It is not a comprehensive account of every experiment from the preceding days. In particular, the earlier Luna pilot had a different controller and protocol and must not be pooled with the local CLLP runs.

The first Qwen generator comparison

The 14 November study trained a local Qwen3-0.6B LoRA for 300 updates and selected checkpoint 220 on validation. Its test set contained 100 contexts from 20 boards. The initial strict-JSON table reported 0/100 valid base outputs because the base model wrapped its JSON in Markdown fences, while the adapter did not. A later, post-hoc symmetric fence-removal check gave 66/100 valid intents for both base and adapter. Using the same bounded search, paths were found for 7/100 base proposals and 44/100 adapter proposals; the base frequently requested the full puzzle goal, so the requests were not matched in difficulty.

That older study also used local_rule, a hand-written current-state baseline. It computes where the player can walk without moving boxes, then selects a legal push, preferring a destination on a goal; if necessary it falls back to a reachable player position. It does not inspect the later demonstration. Its 100/100 valid and solver-reachable proposals mainly show that deliberately choosing a locally feasible request is easy for this rule; they do not show that it plans the complete puzzle. Its graph work should be considered when comparing costs.

11 · Interpretation

What the evidence supports

  • Feasibility: small local models can be fine-tuned to output checked Sokoban intent structures, but the learned proposals remain sensitive to formatting, coordinates and board orientation.
  • Controller measurements: the full-state CLLP results show non-trivial reachability failures and dependency on the target pool. They do not establish how well an intent-conditioned controller would execute Qwen’s proposals.
  • Relative scale: the saved analyses describe empirical profiles and their sensitivity. They do not compare a relative-scale-conditioned generator with otherwise matched unscaled or absolute-duration models.
  • Planning: the completed task results are a useful full-state baseline. They do not validate the proposal’s policy-relative intent search or demonstrate transfer to unseen checkpoint training distributions.

A direct next measurement would evaluate the generator’s own proposals with a fixed, verified intent-conditioned controller, first establishing enough repeated successful candidates per context to estimate scale. Only then can a matched comparison separate demonstration supervision, controller-informed selection, absolute duration and relative scale.

Uncertainty and scope. Results use previously examined boards, different board-source distributions and, for the new LoRAs, one training seed. Board-bootstrap intervals account for clustering by original board within the reported cohort; they do not remove those limitations.
